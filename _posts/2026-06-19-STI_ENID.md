The paper "Dimensions of Diversity and Their Effects on Citation Impact: Evidence from Philosophy of Science", which I co-authored with my master student Guglielmo Sproloquio, has been accepted for presentation at the [30th Annual International Conference on Science and Technology Indicators](https://www.uantwerpen.be/en/conferences/30th-annual-international-conference-on-science-and-technology-indicators/)! The paper is our first application of [SOPHIS](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11192-026-05591-4) data to address a research question: do publications authored by diverse teams in philosophy of science attract more citations than publications authored by homogeneous teams?

The conference, themed **Indicators for a Responsible Research Future**, will take place at the University of Antwerp (Belgium) from 9 to 11 September 2026 and wil bring together leading experts, researchers, and policymakers to explore how science, technology, and innovation (STI) indicators can advance responsible research and innovation (RRI). Key topics will include diversity, inclusivity, openness, and sustainability in research assessment and policy.  

Here's the abstract of the paper:

> *Diversity is becoming a central concern for science policy, both as a matter of equity and as a potential driver of innovation. However, existing scientometric studies on diversity have mainly focused on natural and social sciences, leaving the humanities underexplored. This paper addresses this gap by focusing on philosophy of science, a field in which the epistemic benefits of diversity have been extensively theorized. Using data from the SOPHIS database and OpenAlex, we examine three dimensions of intra-team diversity—gender, institutional, and disciplinary—and their association with citation impact in philosophy of science. Results show that only disciplinary diversity is positively and significantly associated with citation impact. Interaction analyses further reveal that diversity dimensions do not operate independently. While diversity is often promoted as a general driver of scientific excellence, our findings suggest that the impact of diversity is dimension-specific and context-dependent.*
